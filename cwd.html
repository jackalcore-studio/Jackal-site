CWD

Chronic Wasting Disease

You may have heard it called “zombie deer disease,” a dramatic, imprecise nickname apparently designed to turn a serious wildlife disease into a Halloween headline.

Chronic wasting disease is a contagious, progressive, and fatal neurological disease that affects members of the deer family. It is caused by infectious misfolded proteins called prions.

CWD, basically

What is chronic wasting disease?

Chronic wasting disease is a prion disease found in cervids: animals in the deer family. It damages the brain and nervous system and is always fatal once an animal is infected.

The disease usually develops slowly. An infected animal may appear healthy for a long time while still carrying and shedding infectious prions. Later signs can include severe weight loss, poor coordination, unusual behavior, drooling, lowered head posture, increased thirst, and reduced awareness of its surroundings.

Those late-stage signs helped inspire the “zombie deer” label, but the nickname is misleading. The animals are not aggressive movie monsters. They are neurologically ill, physically deteriorating, and in need of a response based on welfare and evidence rather than spectacle.

There is currently no vaccine or treatment that can cure CWD. Management therefore focuses on surveillance, testing, limiting spread, and reducing avoidable exposure.

In ordinary language

CWD is a fatal brain disease in deer-family animals. It can spread before an animal looks sick, and once it is established in an area, controlling it is extremely difficult.

The weird part

Prions.

Not a virus. Not a bacterium. Not a parasite. Somehow, the answer is “a protein folded incorrectly,” because biology enjoys being difficult.

🧬

01 / WHAT IT IS

What is a prion?

Proteins are long chains of amino acids that fold into particular three-dimensional shapes. Their shape helps determine what they do.

A prion is an abnormally folded form of a naturally occurring prion protein. The abnormal form can interact with normal prion proteins and encourage them to adopt the abnormal shape too. Those misfolded proteins accumulate, damage nervous tissue, and create a self-propagating chain reaction.

Less wordy

One badly folded protein bumps into normal proteins and makes them fold badly too. Then those proteins repeat the process.

🍂

02 / HOW IT MOVES

How does CWD spread?

Infected cervids can shed CWD prions in saliva, urine, and feces, including during the long period before obvious symptoms appear. Prions are also present in infected tissues.

Transmission can occur through direct contact between animals or indirectly through contaminated environments. Soil, plants, food, water, dust, equipment, and other surfaces may become part of the transmission pathway.

Prions are unusually resistant to breakdown. They do not behave like ordinary living pathogens, so common disinfecting, decomposition, and cooking processes cannot be assumed to destroy them.

Less wordy

An infected animal can leave prions behind. Other deer may then encounter them through the animal itself or through the place where it lived, fed, drank, or traveled.

Location check

Does my state have CWD?

Possibly. CWD has been detected across a large and expanding part of North America, but its documented distribution changes as new surveillance and testing results are reported.

A state shown without a confirmed case is not automatically a state without CWD. It may mean that the disease has not been detected, testing has been limited, or current data have not yet identified it.

Use a current national map for the broad picture, then check your state or provincial wildlife agency for local rules, testing locations, carcass-transport restrictions, and the newest county information.

Host range

What animals are affected?

PRION DISEASES GENERALLY

Different prion diseases affect different species.

“Prion disease” is a category, not one single illness. Examples include scrapie in sheep and goats, bovine spongiform encephalopathy in cattle, and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease and other prion diseases in humans.

A prion disease adapted to one species does not automatically behave the same way in another. Species barriers, genetics, and prion strains all affect whether transmission occurs and how efficiently it happens.

CWD SPECIFICALLY

CWD affects cervids.

Cervids are members of the deer family. Confirmed susceptible species include several kinds of deer, elk, moose, reindeer, and muntjac. The exact species involved varies by region.

CWD does not appear to naturally infect cattle, other livestock, or household pets. That does not make environmental control unimportant; it describes what has been observed so far.

A careful distinction

What about people?

No confirmed human cases of CWD have been reported. Researchers and public-health agencies continue to study the possibility of a species barrier being crossed. The CDC recommends that hunters in areas with CWD strongly consider testing harvested animals and not eat meat from an animal that tests positive.

“No confirmed cases” means exactly that. It is neither evidence of an active human epidemic nor permission to replace caution with certainty.
