Something looks wrong

What should I do if I find a possibly sick deer?

Do not chase it, corner it, feed it, touch it, move it, or attempt amateur veterinary medicine in the woods. A photograph from a safe distance and an accurate location are more useful than getting close.

01

Keep your distance.

Keep people and pets away. Do not handle saliva, tissue, bodily fluids, attached antlers, or an abscess.

02

Record what you can safely observe.

Note the location, date, species if known, behavior, visible injuries or lumps, and whether the animal is alive or dead. Photos or video can help when taken from a distance.

03

Contact the relevant wildlife authority.

Report it to your state, provincial, territorial, or national wildlife agency. Use local non-emergency dispatch only for an immediate road or public-safety hazard.

04

Let professionals decide what testing is appropriate.

CWD cannot be reliably diagnosed by looking at an animal. Other diseases, injuries, toxins, parasites, and starvation can create overlapping signs.

Similar-looking is not same-disease

CWD versus “cheese disease.”

Caseous lymphadenitis is sometimes nicknamed “cheese disease” because affected lymph nodes can contain thick, caseous material. It is a bacterial disease associated with Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis. It is not a prion disease.

CHRONIC WASTING DISEASE

🧠

Neurological prion disease

  • Caused by infectious misfolded proteins
  • Progressive weight loss and neurological decline
  • May involve stumbling, drooling, lowered head, and unusual behavior
  • No vaccine or curative treatment currently exists
  • Confirmed through specialized postmortem testing

CASEOUS LYMPHADENITIS

🦠

Bacterial abscess disease

  • Usually associated with C. pseudotuberculosis
  • Can cause external or internal abscesses
  • Visible swellings may occur near lymph nodes or beneath the skin
  • Diagnosis requires veterinary or laboratory assessment
  • Treatment decisions belong to wildlife or veterinary professionals
⚠️

Do not squeeze, drain, cut, or sample a lump yourself.

An abscess can contain infectious material, and a lump may have many possible causes. Even when something looks more like a bacterial abscess than CWD, the correct public response is distance, documentation, and professional reporting—not forest surgery.

Do something useful

Ways to get involved.

You do not have to become a full-time prion researcher. Pick a useful action that fits your time, access, and community.

📄

01 / MAKE

Print or make a zine.

DEERCORE zines will eventually be available to download, print, fold, and share. Making your own CWD zines is also highly encouraged. Local information made by actual local people can reach audiences a generic national poster never will.

Cite claims, date maps, include reporting contacts, and mark uncertainty clearly.

📚

02 / SHARE

Put accurate material where people already are.

Ask permission to leave resources at libraries, community boards, outdoor shops, colleges, wildlife centers, veterinary offices, nature groups, or hunting and conservation events.

🗺️

03 / LOCALIZE

Learn your region’s system.

Find out who accepts reports, where hunters can test animals, whether testing is free, which carcass-transport rules apply, and how quickly local maps are updated.

💬

04 / TALK

Correct misinformation without creating new misinformation.

Explain that CWD is serious without presenting every thin deer as infected or claiming human transmission has been confirmed. Good outreach increases concern and accuracy at the same time.

🧪

05 / SUPPORT

Follow and support credible work.

Share laboratory updates, attend public talks, support conservation organizations, respond to public consultations, and direct donations toward transparent research or wildlife programs when possible.

✉️

06 / REPORT BACK

Tell DEERCORE what escaped into the wild.

Future newsletter updates can track where zines were shared, useful local resources, questions people repeatedly ask, and gaps the next outreach project should address.

“Why would me doing anything matter?”

Because public attention is infrastructure.

A person learning about CWD does not cure it. But public understanding changes what happens around the science.

It can increase reports of sick animals, participation in testing, compliance with transport rules, demand for updated surveillance, attendance at public meetings, support for laboratories, and pressure on institutions to explain their work clearly.

Outreach also reveals where systems fail. If people repeatedly cannot find a testing site, understand a map, distinguish CWD from another illness, or report an animal without making six phone calls, that is useful evidence that the response needs repair.

One person does not solve CWD.One person can make the next person notice, report, test, donate, research, print, vote, or ask a better question.
